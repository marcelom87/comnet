## Project Overview

Digital games have proven to be the most popular and broad category of computer applications, generating an entertainment market estimated at billions of dollars annually. Currently, game development significantly drives the advancement of technology in practically all areas of computing (computer graphics, human-machine interface, artificial intelligence, modeling of physical and biological systems, computer architecture, software engineering, signal processing, etc.) [1]. The ability to play, or solve games and problems, is inherent to human beings. Simulating this human capacity is, to this day, one of the greatest challenges in the area of ​​robotics [2]. The development of technologies that allow robots to acquire the ability to analyze context, make decisions as well as precisely move instruments is of great importance for progress towards the automation of tasks that are inappropriate or impossible for humans. In this context, the gaming solution presents itself as a playful, innovative and mainly motivating way to develop human and technological resources in the areas of artificial intelligence, computer vision and robotics. The Guitar Hero game, released in 2005 for video games, was a huge success among teenagers and young people at the end of the last decade. This success is mainly due to proposing a new interface between the human being and the video game, through a device that simulates a real guitar. Several research groups took advantage of this motivation to develop robots that simulate the movements and decisions made by the player [3-10]. In the Department of Computer Science, this line of research has generated several works and graduation projects. A prototype robot, called Grybot [4], was built and is constantly being improved. Is this robot prototype, controlled by FPGA (Field Programmable Gate Array), capable of successfully playing the game Guitar Hero III? Legends of Rock (GH3) [3], released in 2007 for the PlayStation 2 console. In this project, all image analysis and processing and activation of the solenoids responsible for pressing the console control buttons are implemented directly in hardware, using the Verilog hardware description language [5]. This methodology eliminates the use of a computer as a control agent, allowing new adaptive and evolutionary techniques for creating digital systems to be developed and tested in a motivating environment.
