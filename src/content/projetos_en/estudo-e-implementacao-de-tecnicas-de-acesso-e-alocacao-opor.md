## Project Overview

The increase in demand for wireless connectivity, as a result of the popularization of devices that use this technology, has come up against a serious obstacle: the unlicensed bands, known as ISM (Industrial, Scientific and Medical), used for wireless communication, are increasingly congested. In contrast to this scenario, the licensed bands - which aim to meet specific demands and are statically allocated by regulatory agencies, in our country, by ANATEL - are, in certain periods of time and regions, idle, in the order of 5% of use, for example, for the 3Ghz and 5GHz bands, according to a study carried out by the American agency Federal Communications Commission (FCC). It is in this scenario that the discussion of techniques that allow better use of the electromagnetic spectrum arose, where new users (secondary users), or users from congested bands, could explore possible opportunities found in spectrum bands that are momentarily idle. This type of access is known as Opportunistic Spectrum Access. In this context, users who already have the exclusive right to access the proprietary channel (primary users) are considered priority and any communications made by new users must, obligatorily, respect and not interfere with their communications. Basically, the traditional access behavior of primary users is not affected by secondary users who want access to the wireless channel. This is a new area of ​​research and is gaining more and more strength as it presents better use of the spectrum and, therefore, minimizes the problem of scarcity of the electromagnetic spectrum. Primary users continue to use their frequency bands in the usual way and start to "sublease" the unused portion so that secondary users can have access to the spectrum by paying for its use. To make opportunistic access possible, the secondary node uses software-defined radio (SDR) mechanisms. Depending on the level of sophistication of the SDR device, several parameters can be adjusted, such as frequency, modulation scheme, transmission power, among others. When the SDR device is able to adapt to the radio frequency according to pre-defined objectives, such as availability and performance, this device is called cognitive radio (CR). Ensuring spectrum sharing in a way that preserves primary user communications is not a trivial task. Initially, for this opportunistic access to be possible, the secondary user must have efficient mechanisms to identify and use transmission opportunities. In a distributed scenario, these mechanisms imply the secondary user's ability to monitor a set of licensed bands and find a subset of free bands for communication. Once this subset is identified, which must be common between the transmitter and receiver, communication can be carried out. Carrying out these activities in a timely manner to meet delay, delay and flow variation restrictions, in addition to keeping energy consumption within acceptable limits, has proven to be a major challenge. Similarly, routing and media access protocols that meet the needs and characteristics of users in a dynamic access environment need to be developed or even adapted for this new context.
